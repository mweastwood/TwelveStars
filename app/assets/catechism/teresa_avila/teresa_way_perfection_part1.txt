The Way of Perfection: Vol. I
Camino de Perfección: The Way of Prayer & Evangelical Counsels
By St. Teresa of Ávila (Trans. The Benedictines of Stanbrook, 1911)

Chapter 1
The Reason which Moved Me to Found This Monastery in Such Strict Observance
1:1 At the beginning of the foundation of this monastery of St. Joseph of Ávila, it was not my intention to introduce so much external austerity, nor did I think of having no revenues, but rather desired that the community should have sufficient means to avoid anxiety about temporal necessities.
1:2 But at that time news reached me of the great harm and havoc that the Lutherans and other heretics were causing in France, and how this unhappy sect was increasing daily. This news afflicted me exceedingly, and, as if I could do something or were someone, I wept before the Lord and besought Him to remedy so great an evil. (Psalm 118:136)
1:3 It seemed to me that I would have given a thousand lives to save one of the many souls that were being lost. Seeing myself a woman, and weak, and unable to serve God as I desired in the active field of the Church, all my longing was, and still is, that since He has so many enemies and so few friends, these few friends should be good ones.
1:4 Therefore I resolved to do that little which was in my power, which is to follow the Evangelical Counsels with all the perfection I could, and to induce the few sisters who are here to do the same, trusting in the great goodness of God, Who never fails to assist those who determine to leave all things for His love. (Matthew 19:27-29)
1:5 For when the world is in flames, and the enemies of the Cross of Christ seek to destroy His Church and defile His sanctuaries, we who have consecrated ourselves to Him must remain within our cloister, interceding continually for the defenders of the Faith and for the preachers and theologians who fight upon the spiritual battlefield. (Exodus 17:11-12)

Chapter 2
How the Temporal Needs of the Body Must Be Disregarded and the Blessing of Poverty
2:1 Do not think, my sisters, that because you do not seek after worldly goods or honors, you are therefore exempt from all care; for if you do not fix your eyes solely on God, temporal anxieties will easily find a way to disturb your recollection and peace of mind.
2:2 I tell you that true poverty brings with it a glorious freedom and dominion over all created things. When a soul has resolved to possess nothing on earth, God Himself becomes its inheritance and rich treasure. (Psalm 15:5; Matthew 6:31-33)
2:3 Why should we fear lack of food or clothing when our Spouse has promised that if we seek first the Kingdom of God and His justice, all these things shall be added unto us? He Who feeds the birds of the air and clothes the lilies of the field will never abandon His brides who have cast all their trust upon His divine providence. (Luke 12:24-31)
2:4 The less we have in this life, the less we have to account for at the hour of death, and the lighter will be our journey toward the heavenly homeland. Let us rejoice in being poor, in being despised, and in lacking what is superfluous, for our Lord Himself had not where to lay His head. (Matthew 8:20)
2:5 Never let there be among you any discussion or solicitude regarding money or provisions; let the Prioress and the Procuratrix care for what is strictly necessary, while the rest remain in holy silence, attending only to prayer, praise, and the love of God.

Chapter 3
The Motive for Undertaking This Strict Life: Intercession for the Church and Her Defenders
3:1 O my sisters in Christ, help me to beseech this of our Lord, for this is the sole reason why He has gathered you together in this house. This is your holy vocation; these are to be your constant desires; to this end your tears and prayers must be directed.
3:2 Do not spend your prayer in asking for worldly prosperity, or for health, or for honors for your relatives, for such petitions do not belong to the perfection of our state. But pray with all the fervor of your hearts for the Supreme Pontiff, for the bishops, for the priests, and for all those who labor in defending the truth of the Catholic Church against heresy. (1 Timothy 2:1-4)
3:3 If your prayers, desires, disciplines, and fasts are not offered for this intention, know that you are not fulfilling the purpose for which our Lord brought you together in this monastery of St. Joseph.
3:4 When the holy captains and soldiers of the Church are engaged in preaching and defending the truth, they undergo immense labors and perils; it is only just that we who are sheltered in the cloister should sustain them by our continuous prayers and sacrifices, just as Aaron and Hur supported the hands of Moses upon the mount. (Exodus 17:12)
3:5 In this manner, though we remain hidden from the world, we shall cooperate in the salvation of countless souls and assist our Mother the Church in her greatest necessities.

Chapter 4
Exhortation to the Sisters to Keep Their Rule and the Three Chief Foundations: Mutual Love, Detachment, and Humility
4:1 Now that you understand the noble end for which our Lord has brought you to this house, consider what virtues are necessary to attain it. It is not enough to wear the habit or to observe the enclosure if the interior virtues are absent from our souls.
4:2 There are three virtues which are the principal foundations of our religious life, and without which it is impossible to lead a life of true prayer and perfection.
4:3 The first of these is mutual love and charity among the sisters, loving one another as Christ has loved us, without partiality or self-interest. (John 13:34-35)
4:4 The second is detachment from all created things, that is, a complete interior freedom from the world, from our families, and even from ourselves and our own will. (Luke 14:33)
4:5 The third, and the most necessary of all, is true humility, which is the foundation and support of all the other virtues, and without which the spiritual edifice will quickly crumble to pieces. (Proverbs 16:18-19)
4:6 If you diligently cultivate these three virtues, you will easily preserve the peace of your souls, your prayer will be fruitful, and you will make rapid progress along the way of perfection.

Chapter 5
On Detachment from Relatives and the Freedom of the Solitary Heart
5:1 Let us speak now of detachment from our relatives and kindred, for this is a matter of great importance for those who have embraced the religious state.
5:2 When a soul leaves the world to enter the cloister, it must leave behind all excessive attachment to flesh and blood, remembering the words of the Holy Ghost in the Psalm: "Hear, O daughter, and see, and incline thy ear: and forget thy people and thy father's house." (Psalm 44:11)
5:3 Excessive conversation and intercourse with relatives only serves to bring the dust and turmoil of the world into the quiet of the monastery, filling the mind with worldly news, anxieties, and distractions that extinguish the spirit of prayer.
5:4 We must love our parents and relatives in God and for God, praying earnestly for their spiritual welfare and eternal salvation, but without allowing their temporal concerns or affections to entangle our hearts. (Matthew 10:37)
5:5 A religious who is still attached to her family is like a bird tied by a cord: though the cord be slender, she cannot fly freely toward the heights of divine contemplation until it is broken.

Chapter 6
The Nature and Excellence of Mutual Love Among Religious and Perfect Charity
6:1 Now concerning mutual love among the sisters: this is of such great consequence that I cannot insist upon it too strongly. "By this shall all men know that you are my disciples, if you have love one for another." (John 13:35)
6:2 Where true charity reigns, there is no room for envy, jealousy, or bitterness; each one rejoices in the virtues and graces bestowed upon her sisters as though they were her own.
6:3 Perfect love is not founded upon natural attraction, pleasing manners, or physical appearance, for such affections are frail and easily change; but it is founded upon the love of God, seeing in every sister the image of Christ and a temple of the Holy Spirit. (1 Corinthians 3:16)
6:4 In this monastery, where there are so few sisters, all must be friends, all must love one another, all must help one another, and all must bear with one another's infirmities and defects with great patience and gentleness. (Colossians 3:12-14)
6:5 If you see a sister fall into any imperfection, do not judge her or speak of it to others, but cover it with the mantle of charity, pray for her, and gently encourage her to virtue.

Chapter 7
How Spiritual Love Differs from Worldly Affection and Avoids Particular Friendships
7:1 You must be on your guard against particular friendships and exclusive attachments, for these are like a pestilence in a small community.
7:2 When two or three sisters form a separate intimacy, they easily become blind to each other's faults, neglect the common good of the community, and cause disunion and sadness among the rest.
7:3 Spiritual love is universal; it embraces everyone in the community with equal kindness, desiring for all the highest perfection and union with God.
7:4 Worldly love seeks its own satisfaction and consolation, whereas spiritual love seeks only the will of God and the spiritual profit of the neighbor, even at the cost of its own convenience. (1 Corinthians 13:4-7)
7:5 Let your conversation be simple, modest, and holy; when you speak with one another, let it be of God, of the beauty of virtue, and of the happiness of serving so good a Master.

Chapter 8
On the Great Benefit of Total Detachment from All Created Things for the Love of the Creator
8:1 It may seem to you that total detachment from everything created is a very hard and austere requirement; but if you consider the immense gain that comes from it, you will count all that you leave behind as mere refuse. (Philippians 3:8)
8:2 For what is all the world, and all its riches, pleasures, and honors, when compared to the possession of the Creator of the universe? The soul that clings to anything outside of God is enslaved by that thing, whereas the detached soul is absolute mistress over the whole world.
8:3 When we give up our own will, our own judgments, and our desire for earthly satisfaction, our Lord immediately fills the void in our heart with His divine presence and peace which surpasses all understanding. (Philippians 4:7)
8:4 He does not give Himself entirely to us until we have given ourselves entirely to Him. This is a divine exchange: we give Him our poverty, our weakness, and our nothingness, and He gives us His infinite riches, His strength, and His own divinity.
8:5 Let us therefore strip ourselves of all things with joy and resolution, running without hindrance along the royal road of the Holy Cross. (Matthew 16:24)

Chapter 9
On the Great Good of Renouncing Worldly Honors, Nobility, and Precedence
9:1 O my daughters, how ridiculous and pitiable are the points of honor, nobility, and precedence which so greatly trouble worldly men!
9:2 In this house of St. Joseph, let there never be heard any mention of nobility, high birth, or worldly distinction. For we are all daughters of the same Heavenly Father, all redeemed by the same precious Blood of Jesus Christ, and all sisters in religion. (Galatians 3:28)
9:3 He who desires to be first and greatest among us must become the servant and least of all, following the example of our Savior Who came not to be ministered unto, but to minister. (Matthew 20:26-28)
9:4 The only nobility that is recognized in the Kingdom of Heaven is the nobility of virtue, humility, and divine charity. Let us strive to excel only in humbling ourselves below everyone else and in serving our sisters with joyful hearts.
9:5 If anyone feels within herself a temptation to pride on account of her family or former state in the world, let her immediately cast herself at the feet of the lowest sister and perform some humble office in the house, that she may extinguish this spark of the devil's fire.

Chapter 10
On Humility and Mortification in Desiring No Exemption from Common Observances
10:1 Another point of great importance for our spiritual progress is to love the common life and to desire no exemptions or privileges in our religious observances.
10:2 Self-love is very subtle, and under the pretext of indisposition, weakness, or spiritual necessity, it often seeks to obtain exemptions from choir, from fasting, or from the common labors of the community.
10:3 We must practice mortification not only in great things, but especially in the small, daily opportunities of life: in accepting the food that is set before us, in bearing cold and heat without complaint, and in conforming ourselves in everything to the common rule.
10:4 True humility makes a soul consider itself the most unworthy of all the sisters, and therefore it is astonished that the others tolerate its presence and supply its needs. (Ephesians 3:8)
10:5 When a religious possesses this disposition, she finds sweetness in every humiliation, peace in every trial, and a sure safeguard against all the illusions of the enemy.

Chapter 11
On Sickness, Bodily Frailty, and How the Fear of Losing Health Hinders Spiritual Progress
11:1 Many souls make little progress in prayer and virtue because they are continually terrified of losing their health or injuring their delicate bodies.
11:2 They spend their whole lives studying their ailments, seeking remedies, and pampering the flesh, so that the body becomes the master and the soul remains a miserable slave.
11:3 I do not say that we should neglect necessary care when we are truly ill, for obedience and charity require that the sick be treated with great compassion and diligence; but I warn you against that excessive fear which imagines sickness where none exists.
11:4 Remember that our bodies are made of clay and must soon return to dust. What does it matter if this poor body suffers a little in the service of God, since our Lord suffered so much for our redemption? (Romans 8:18; 2 Corinthians 4:16-17)
11:5 It is far better to die striving to advance in virtue and penance than to live many years in lukewarmness and self-indulgence. Let us place our health entirely in the hands of God, and He will grant us sufficient strength to accomplish His holy will.

Chapter 12
How the True Servant of God Must Despise Life and Seek Only the Cross
12:1 The true servant of God must resolve to despise this mortal life, knowing that we are pilgrims and strangers upon earth, seeking a better country, which is Heaven. (Hebrews 11:13-16)
12:2 If we desire to follow Jesus Christ, we must take up His Cross daily and not seek our own ease or consolation. The Cross is the royal gate through which all the saints have entered into glory. (Luke 9:23)
12:3 When trials, persecutions, or interior desolations come upon us, we should receive them with thanksgiving as precious gifts from the hand of our heavenly Father, Who chasteneth every son whom He receiveth. (Hebrews 12:6)
12:4 He who loves the Cross finds in it a hidden sweetness which the world cannot understand; for where suffering is borne for love of Christ, there divine grace abounds in greatest abundance. (2 Corinthians 12:9-10)
12:5 Let us not seek an easy or comfortable way to Heaven, for our Lord Himself entered not into His glory until He had suffered upon Calvary. (Luke 24:26)

Chapter 13
On the Practice of Humility and Bearing Unjust Reproaches without Excusing Ourselves
13:1 A great test of true humility is to bear reproaches, blame, and accusations without seeking to excuse ourselves, even when we are entirely innocent.
13:2 Our natural pride immediately rises up to defend our innocence and preserve our good reputation; but if we look at our Savior Jesus Christ, we see that when He was falsely accused before Pilate and Herod, He answered not a word. (Matthew 27:12-14; 1 Peter 2:23)
13:3 What great harm is done to us if someone thinks ill of us? We are far worse in the sight of God on account of our hidden sins and negligences than anyone could ever imagine us to be.
13:4 By remaining silent when accused, we gain a great victory over self-love, practice heroic humility, and draw down upon our souls the special favor and blessing of God.
13:5 Of course, if keeping silence would cause scandal or harm to others or to the community, charity and obedience require that we explain the truth simply and without anger; but when it concerns only our own personal humiliation, let us embrace it with joy for the love of Christ.

Chapter 14
On the Perfection of the Evangelical Counsels and Following Christ in Poverty and Humiliation
14:1 The Evangelical Counsels—voluntary poverty, perpetual chastity, and entire obedience—are the narrow path traced by our divine Redeemer for those who wish to follow Him most closely. (Matthew 19:21)
14:2 By poverty we consecrate all external goods to God; by chastity we consecrate our bodies and affections; and by obedience we offer the most precious sacrifice of all, which is our own will and intellect.
14:3 He who obeys his superior obeys God Himself, for our Lord has said: "He that heareth you, heareth me." (Luke 10:16)
14:4 In holy obedience there is great peace and security, for the obedient soul does not have to worry about choosing the right path; its superior directs it in the place of God, and it cannot go astray.
14:5 Let us cherish our holy vows as the precious jewels of our spiritual crown, observing them not with mechanical routine, but with ardent love and generosity of heart.

Chapter 15
The Great Gain of Bearing Blame without Being at Fault and the Example of Christ
15:1 I return to this matter of bearing blame without fault, because it is of such immense benefit to the soul that desires to arrive at contemplation.
15:2 It seems very difficult to our nature, but with the help of divine grace it becomes easy and delightful. For when the soul begins to taste the sweetness of being humbled for the love of God, it desires nothing else than to be despised and counted as nothing.
15:3 Consider how our sweet Jesus was called a blasphemer, a drunkard, a friend of publicans, and one possessed by the devil, yet He bore all with infinite patience and meekness for our salvation. (Matthew 11:19; John 8:48)
15:4 Shall we, who are miserable worms of the earth, complain when a slight word is spoken against us? Let us be ashamed of our delicate pride and learn meekness from the Sacred Heart of our Master. (Matthew 11:29)
15:5 The soul that practices this virtue with perseverance will soon find itself freed from all human respect and raised to an intimate union with God.

Chapter 16
The Difference between the Active and Contemplative Life: Martha and Mary
16:1 You must understand, my sisters, that in the spiritual life there are different vocations and offices, just as in the house of Bethany there were Martha and Mary. (Luke 10:38-42)
16:2 Martha served our Lord with great diligence in external works of charity, preparing what was necessary for His bodily nourishment; while Mary sat at His feet, listening in sweet contemplation to His divine words.
16:3 Both lives are good and holy, and both are necessary in the Church of God. Those who are called to the active life must not despise the contemplatives, nor must contemplatives look down upon those who labor in external ministries.
16:4 Indeed, the most perfect life is that which unites both, where the soul, like Martha, serves Christ in His members, and like Mary, rests in His divine presence.
16:5 If our Lord does not give you the gift of high contemplation, do not be disquieted, but serve Him faithfully with Martha in humility, obedience, and the performance of common duties, and He will reward you no less generously.

Chapter 17
How Not All Souls Are Called to Contemplation and How Humility Reaches the Same Goal
17:1 It is a great mistake to think that because a soul does not enjoy supernatural contemplation or sensible sweetness in prayer, it cannot attain high sanctity.
17:2 Contemplation is a free and gratuitous gift of God, which He gives to whom He pleases, when He pleases, and as He pleases, without any merit on our part. (1 Corinthians 12:11)
17:3 But perfection consists in having our will completely conformed to the will of God, loving what He loves and embracing what He ordains. (John 4:34)
17:4 A soul that practices deep humility, exact obedience, and perfect charity, even though it never experiences any mystical favors, may be far more pleasing to God and have a higher place in Heaven than one that receives great visions and revelations.
17:5 Therefore, let no one lose heart or give up prayer because she finds herself dry and helpless; let her remain at the foot of the Cross in humility, and she will reach the harbor of salvation in safety.

Chapter 18
How Mental Prayer is Open to All and God Gives Refreshment to the Thirsty
18:1 Let all understand that the road of mental prayer is open to everyone, and our Lord invites all without exception to come and drink from the fountain of living water. (John 7:37-38; Isaiah 55:1)
18:2 He says: "If any man thirst, let him come to me and drink." He does not say: "Let some come and others stay away," but He calls everyone: the rich and the poor, the wise and the simple, the healthy and the infirm.
18:3 For God is infinite goodness, and He desires to communicate His divine life and riches to all His creatures who sincerely seek Him.
18:4 All that is required is that we have a true and burning thirst for this living water, and that we set out upon the journey with resolute determination, never turning back on account of difficulties, aridities, or temptations.
18:5 Having laid these firm foundations of humility, detachment, and mutual charity in this first part of our treatise, we shall now proceed to consider the sublime practice of prayer and the contemplation of the Lord's Prayer.
