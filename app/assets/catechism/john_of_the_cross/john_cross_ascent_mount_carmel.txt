Ascent of Mount Carmel
Subida del Monte Carmelo
By St. John of the Cross (Trans. David Lewis, Rev. Benedict Zimmerman, O.C.D.)

Chapter 1
Prologue: The Stanzas of the Soul and the Scope of the Active Ascent
1:1 On an obscure night, kindled in love with yearnings—oh, happy chance!—I went forth unobserved, my house being now at rest.
1:2 In darkness and secure, by the secret ladder, disguised—oh, happy chance!—in darkness and in concealment, my house being now at rest.
1:3 In the happy night, in secret, when none saw me, nor I beheld aught, without light or guide, save that which burned in my heart.
1:4 This light guided me more surely than the light of noonday to the place where He was waiting for me—a place where none appeared.
1:5 O night that guided me, O night more lovely than the dawn, O night that joined Beloved with lover, lover transformed in the Beloved!
1:6 Upon my flowery breast, kept wholly for Himself alone, there He stayed sleeping, and I caressed Him, and the fanning of the cedars made a breeze.
1:7 The breeze blew from the turret as I parted His locks; with His gentle hand He wounded my neck and caused all my senses to be suspended.
1:8 I remained, lost in oblivion; my face I reclined on the Beloved. All ceased and I abandoned myself, leaving my cares forgotten among the lilies.
1:9 The entire doctrine which I propose to treat in this Ascent of Mount Carmel is included in the foregoing stanzas, which describe the journey of a soul to the summit of the mountain of perfection, which is the high state of union with God.
1:10 To attain to this state, a soul must pass through two primary kinds of night, which spiritual writers call the active and passive purifications of the senses and of the spirit. Here we treat first of the active night, wherein the soul, aided by divine grace, labors to strip and purify itself of all disordered affections, attachments, and natural operations. (Matthew 7:14; Luke 14:33)

Chapter 2
Book I — Chapter 1: The Dark Night and the Twofold Active Purgation of Sense and Spirit
2:1 A soul that aspires to reach the state of perfection must pass through two principal nights of spiritual purgation: the night of the senses and the night of the spirit.
2:2 The night of the senses is common to beginners, wherein the exterior and interior sensory appetites are purged and brought into subjection to the spirit. The night of the spirit is proper to the proficient, wherein the spiritual faculties—intellect, memory, and will—are stripped and purified of all natural and supernatural attachments, so that they may be united with God in pure faith, hope, and charity.
2:3 We call this journey a night for three reasons: first, because the starting point is the renunciation of all worldly desires, which leaves the appetites in darkness; second, because the means and road to union is Faith, which is as dark as night to the human intellect; third, because the term and end, which is God, is incomprehensible and exceeds the natural light of every creature. (Psalm 18:11; 1 Corinthians 13:12)

Chapter 3
Book I — Chapter 2: The Nature of the Night of Sense and Mortification of Appetites
3:1 By the night of the senses we do not understand the mere absence of material things, for the soul is not defiled by things in themselves, but by the appetite and longing for them.
3:2 When the soul renounces and mortifies its appetites for all sensible objects, it remains in darkness and emptiness as regards its natural senses, just as the eye is in darkness when all light is withdrawn.
3:3 This privation is rightly called a dark night, for the senses are rendered blind and dead to the world, so that the soul may live exclusively to God. (Colossians 3:3; Galatians 6:14)

Chapter 4
Book I — Chapter 3: Detachment from Sensual Objects and Privation of Desire
4:1 The reason why the soul must enter this night of sense is that all created things, compared with the infinite being of God, are nothingness.
4:2 He who sets his heart and affection upon creatures makes himself like unto them, and therefore falls from the supreme dignity of God into nothingness.
4:3 For love creates equality and likeness between the lover and the object loved. He who loves a creature remains as low and base as that creature, and can never attain to divine transformation until all such attachments are completely eradicated. (Psalm 49:20; 1 John 2:15-17)

Chapter 5
Book I — Chapter 4: The Necessity of Passing Through the Dark Night to Divine Union
5:1 Two contraries cannot co-exist in one and the same subject. Light and darkness, the spirit of God and the spirit of the world, are absolute contraries. (2 Corinthians 6:14)
5:2 Hence, affection for the creature and affection for God cannot abide together in the human heart.
5:3 Until the sensory appetites are extinguished and silenced, the divine wisdom and supernatural light cannot enter and transform the soul. (Wisdom 1:4; Matthew 6:24)

Chapter 6
Book I — Chapter 5: Affection to Creatures Renders the Soul Incapable of Divine Transformation
6:1 The presence of any disordered desire hinders the operation of the Holy Ghost in the soul.
6:2 Just as a spotless pane of glass receives the rays of the sun and is transformed into light, while a stained pane remains dark and opaque, so the clean soul is transformed into God, while the impure soul remains in darkness.
6:3 Therefore, the soul that desires to be united with God must cleanse itself of every speck of creaturely attachment, so that the divine ray may shine through it without obstacle. (Ephesians 5:27; Matthew 5:8)

Chapter 7
Book I — Chapter 6: The Harm Caused by Unmortified Appetites: Weariness and Torment
7:1 Unmortified appetites inflict five great injuries upon the soul: they weary it, torment it, blind it, defile it, and weaken it.
7:2 First, they weary the soul, keeping it in perpetual unrest, like an insatiable child weeping for what cannot satisfy it. (Isaiah 55:2; Habakkuk 2:5)
7:3 Second, they torment the soul with anguish and burning, like thorns that pierce the heart and cause continuous agony until they are plucked out. (Psalm 38:8; 1 Timothy 6:9-10)

Chapter 8
Book I — Chapter 7: How Appetites Blind and Darken the Understanding
8:1 The third injury is blindness and intellectual darkness. Just as a cloud obscures the brightness of the sun, so disordered affections obscure the natural judgment of the intellect.
8:2 When the eye of the soul is covered with the dust of worldly desires, it cannot discern the truth of divine things nor understand the commandments of God. (Deuteronomy 28:29; Ephesians 4:18)
8:3 It mistakes evil for good and darkness for light, falling into countless errors and illusions. (Isaiah 5:20)

Chapter 9
Book I — Chapter 8: How Appetites Pollute and Defile the Soul
9:1 The fourth injury is pollution and spiritual uncleanness. The soul, which in the state of grace is a mirror reflecting the beauty of God, becomes stained and filthy through affection for vile creatures.
9:2 As precious gold is tarnished when mixed with base earth, so the noble soul is degraded when it mingles its love with the mire of temporal pleasures. (Lamentations 4:1; Revelation 22:11)
9:3 It loses its heavenly radiance and becomes abhorrent in the sight of the Angels.

Chapter 10
Book I — Chapter 9: How Appetites Weaken the Soul and Cool the Fervor of Virtue
10:1 The fifth injury is weakness and lukewarmness. When the energy and affection of the soul are divided among many creatures, its strength is dissipated and dissolved.
10:2 It lacks the vigor to practice solid virtue, to resist temptation, and to run in the way of God's commandments. (Psalm 119:32; Matthew 26:41)
10:3 Like a tree whose sap is drained by countless suckers, the soul becomes barren of spiritual fruit.

Chapter 11
Book I — Chapter 10: Complete Mortification Required: Total Detachment Even from Minute Desires
11:1 It is not enough to renounce grave and mortal sins; the soul that seeks perfection must mortify all voluntary attachments, even the smallest.
11:2 It matters little whether a bird be tied with a slender thread or a heavy rope, for so long as the thread is not broken, the bird is equally prevented from flying.
11:3 One single habitual attachment to a trifle—a particular habit, a book, a cell, or a person—is sufficient to hold back the soul from reaching the summit of divine union. (Song of Solomon 2:15; James 2:10)

Chapter 12
Book I — Chapter 11: Natural Desires vs Habitual Attachments and Venial Imperfections
12:1 We must distinguish between natural appetites and voluntary attachments. Natural appetites, such as hunger, thirst, and fatigue, do not impede union so long as they remain within the bounds of reason and necessity.
12:2 But voluntary attachments and habitual imperfections, which are embraced with deliberate consent, must be mortified with unflinching resolution.
12:3 When the will withdraws its consent from all disordered movements, the soul enters into that sweet peace which surpasses all understanding. (Philippians 4:7; Romans 8:13)

Chapter 13
Book I — Chapter 12: How the Soul Must Enter the Active Night of Sense
13:1 To enter this active night of sense, the soul must adopt two principal practices: the imitation of Christ and the renunciation of all satisfaction.
13:2 In every action and thought, let the soul consider how Christ our Lord would act, and strive to conform its life to His divine model. (John 13:15; 1 Peter 2:21)
13:3 Let it choose not what is easiest, but what is most arduous; not what is pleasant, but what is unpleasant; not what is consoling, but what is desolating.

Chapter 14
Book I — Chapter 13: Practical Counsels for Conquering the Appetites and Imitating Christ
14:1 In order to conquer the desires of the senses, cultivate always a longing: Not for what is easy, but for what is most difficult;
14:2 Not for what is delectable, but for what is most distasteful; not for what is consoling, but for what is sorrowful;
14:3 Not for what brings rest, but for what brings labor; not for what is high and precious, but for what is lowest and most despised;
14:4 Not for desiring anything, but for desiring nothing. In this path of nothingness (nada), the soul finds everything (todo), because it possesses God, Who is all in all. (1 Corinthians 3:21-23; 2 Corinthians 6:10)

Chapter 15
Book I — Chapter 14: Exposition of the First Stanza: The Departure in Joyful Freedom
15:1 In the first stanza the soul sings of its happy fortune in having escaped from the prison and tyranny of its sensory desires.
15:2 "On an obscure night, kindled in love with yearnings—oh, happy chance!—I went forth unobserved, my house being now at rest."
15:3 The house of sensuality being silenced and at rest, the soul is set free from the bondage of Egypt and begins its journey through the desert to the promised land. (Exodus 14:30; Psalm 124:7)

Chapter 16
Book I — Chapter 15: The House Being Now at Rest: Subjection of the Sensory Appetite
16:1 The house of the soul is the lower, sensory nature with all its passions, appetites, and senses.
16:2 When these are pacified, mortified, and brought into subjection to the reason and spirit, the house is said to be at rest.
16:3 In this stillness, the soul can freely mount upward to God without fear of hindrance or distraction from the flesh. (Romans 6:6; Galatians 5:24)

Chapter 17
Book II — Chapter 1: The Active Night of the Spirit and the Purgation of the Intellect
17:1 We now proceed to the second part of this treatise, which is the active night of the spirit, wherein the spiritual faculties—intellect, memory, and will—are purged and prepared for divine union.
17:2 This night is incomparably darker and more arduous than the night of sense, because it touches the very root and substance of the soul.
17:3 The three faculties are purified by the three theological virtues: the intellect by Faith, the memory by Hope, and the will by Charity. (1 Corinthians 13:13; 1 Thessalonians 1:3)

Chapter 18
Book II — Chapter 2: The Second Part of the Night: Faith as the Dark and Blind Guide
18:1 Faith is the second part of the night, corresponding to midnight, which is the darkest and most obscure period of the journey.
18:2 Faith is called dark because it causes us to believe truths revealed by God Himself which transcend all human reason and surpass the natural capacity of the intellect. (Hebrews 11:1; Isaiah 7:9)
18:3 Therefore, the more light and certainty Faith gives to the soul, the darker and more blind it seems to the natural understanding.

Chapter 19
Book II — Chapter 3: Why Faith is a Dark Night to the Understanding
19:1 Just as the dazzling brightness of the midday sun blinds the eye of an owl, so the infinite light of divine truth blinds and overwhelms the finite intellect of man.
19:2 When the intellect seeks to comprehend God through natural images, concepts, and reasons, it is in darkness; but when it blinds itself to all creatures and leans solely on Faith, it is guided in the truest light.
19:3 Hence David said: "Clouds and darkness are round about Him; justice and judgment are the establishment of His throne." (Psalm 97:2; 1 Kings 8:12)

Chapter 20
Book II — Chapter 4: The General Way of Faith: Stripping the Soul of Natural Conceptions
20:1 To travel safely along the road of Faith, the intellect must be stripped of all distinct and clear concepts concerning God.
20:2 For God cannot be grasped by any human concept, comparison, or imagination. Whatever the mind can conceive or imagine is infinitely distant from what God is in Himself.
20:3 The soul must advance by unknowing (un saber no sabiendo), walking in darkness and leaning with simple trust upon the word of God. (Exodus 20:21; Acts 17:23)

Chapter 21
Book II — Chapter 5: The Transforming Union of the Soul with God by Love and Grace
21:1 Union of the soul with God is twofold: the first is substantial union, by which God sustains the being of all creatures, good and evil alike; the second is the union of likeness and transformation by grace and love.
21:2 This second union takes place when two wills—the will of man and the will of God—are perfectly united, so that there is nothing in the creature contrary to the Creator.
21:3 When the soul is purged of all that is unlike God, the divine light transforms it, so that the soul appears to be God by participation, though retaining its distinct created substance. (1 John 3:2; 2 Peter 1:4; Galatians 2:20)

Chapter 22
Book II — Chapter 6: The Three Theological Virtues Perfecting the Three Spiritual Faculties
22:1 The three theological virtues produce a complete void and darkness in the three spiritual faculties.
22:2 Faith empties the intellect of all natural light and distinct understanding; Hope empties the memory of all possession of created things; Charity empties the will of all affection and pleasure apart from God.
22:3 Thus the soul is made like a blank canvas upon which the Divine Artist may paint His own likeness in pure beauty. (Psalm 73:25; Philippians 3:13-14)

Chapter 23
Book II — Chapter 7: The Narrow Path of the Cross and Total Self-Denial
23:1 Our Lord declared: "If any man will come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me." (Matthew 16:24)
23:2 Many seek spiritual consolations and sweet feelings, but few are willing to embrace the naked cross of total self-denial and annihilation of spirit.
23:3 He who desires to find his life must lose it for Christ's sake, entering through the narrow gate which leads to life eternal. (Matthew 7:13-14; Luke 9:24)

Chapter 24
Book II — Chapter 8: No Created Thing or Knowledge Can Serve as Proximate Means of Union
24:1 Between the creature and the Creator there is an infinite distance and no proportion whatever.
24:2 Neither heaven nor earth, nor angels, nor any distinct knowledge or imaginary representation, can serve as a proximate and direct means of union with God.
24:3 For all created things have limits and boundaries, whereas God is infinite, limitless, and incomprehensible. (Isaiah 40:17-18; Romans 11:33)

Chapter 25
Book II — Chapter 9: Faith as the Sole Proximate and Proportionate Means of Union
25:1 Faith is the only proximate means of union between the intellect and God, because Faith proposes to us God as He is: infinite, three in Persons and one in Essence.
25:2 There is a true proportion between Faith and God, because both are obscure and incomprehensible to the created intellect.
25:3 In this life, the more a soul believes without seeing, the more intimately it is united with the Divine Word. (John 20:29; 2 Corinthians 5:7)

Chapter 26
Book II — Chapter 10: Division of Intellectual Apprehensions: Corporeal vs Spiritual
26:1 Intellectual apprehensions are divided into two general kinds: corporeal and spiritual.
26:2 Corporeal apprehensions enter through the exterior senses or are formed in the imagination; spiritual apprehensions are received directly in the intellect without the intervention of any bodily organ.
26:3 Both kinds must be carefully examined and regulated, so that the soul may not be hindered on its way to pure Faith. (1 Corinthians 2:11-14)

Chapter 27
Book II — Chapter 11: Exterior Corporeal Visions and Apprehensions of the Senses
27:1 Corporeal visions are those which are perceived by the bodily eyes, such as figures of saints, heavenly lights, or celestial sounds heard by the ears.
27:2 The soul must never attach itself to these phenomena, nor desire them, nor accept them without examination, for they are easily counterfeit by the devil. (2 Corinthians 11:14)
27:3 Even if they proceed from God, the profit lies in the spiritual effect produced in the heart, not in the sensible vision, which should always be rejected with humble detachment.

Chapter 28
Book II — Chapter 12: Natural Imaginary Apprehensions and Interior Representations
28:1 Imaginary apprehensions are forms, figures, and images created in the imagination and phantasy during meditation.
28:2 These images are useful and necessary for beginners, to nourish their devotion and assist them in thinking upon the mysteries of Christ.
28:3 But when God calls the soul to a higher state of contemplation, these images must be laid aside, for the soul cannot walk at the same time in sensible forms and in pure spiritual contemplation. (1 Corinthians 13:11)

Chapter 29
Book II — Chapter 13: Three Signs for Leaving Meditation and Passing to Contemplation
29:1 The soul must observe three signs before abandoning meditation and discursive prayer.
29:2 First: when it can no longer meditate or find any fruit or sweetness in the imagination, but experiences only dryness and difficulty.
29:3 Second: when it has no desire to fix its imagination or senses on any other particular object, worldly or spiritual.
29:4 Third: when the soul delights to abide alone with God in loving, peaceful, and quiet attention, without specific thoughts or reflections. (Psalm 46:10; 1 Kings 19:12)

Chapter 30
Book II — Chapter 14: The Nature of Loving Contemplative Attention and Peaceful Rest
30:1 When these three signs are present, the soul must rest in this quiet, loving gaze upon God, without striving to form new concepts or discursive reasoning.
30:2 For God is then communicating His divine light secretly and invisibly to the spirit, as a mother quietly nurses her resting infant. (Psalm 131:2)
30:3 Any effort of the soul to return to active meditation at this time will disturb the divine communication and cause great loss.

Chapter 31
Book II — Chapter 15: When to Return to Meditation and When to Abide in Contemplation
31:1 In the beginning of this transition, the contemplative state is not continuous, but intermittent.
31:2 Therefore, when the loving attention ceases and the soul finds itself distracted, it may return to gentle meditation and pious reflections until the quiet presence of God returns.
31:3 But as soon as the peaceful presence is felt, the soul must immediately desist from all active operations and rest in God alone. (Song of Solomon 3:5)

Chapter 32
Book II — Chapter 16: Supernatural Imaginary Visions and Representations
32:1 Supernatural imaginary visions are those presented to the imagination by divine or angelic agency without the use of the exterior senses.
32:2 Such were the visions of the prophets, as when Isaiah saw the Lord sitting upon a throne high and elevated. (Isaiah 6:1; Ezekiel 1:1)
32:3 Although these visions are noble and supernatural, the soul must remain detached from them, for the essence of divine union consists not in visions, but in faith and love.

Chapter 33
Book II — Chapter 17: Dangers of Imaginary Visions and Why the Soul Must Reject Them
33:1 Attaching oneself to imaginary visions brings many grave perils: spiritual pride, vanity, deception by the devil, and distraction from the true path of faith.
33:2 The devil often transforms himself into an angel of light to deceive spiritual persons with beautiful and sweet visions.
33:3 By rejecting all visions, the soul remains safe from deception, practices humility, and advances straight to God in pure faith. (Colossians 2:18; 1 John 4:1)

Chapter 34
Book II — Chapter 18: Why God Sometimes Grants Visions Even Though We Must Not Desire Them
34:1 Why does God grant supernatural visions if we are commanded to reject them?
34:2 God gives them out of condescension to our human weakness, in order to draw imperfect souls away from gross earthly pleasures and raise them to spiritual realities.
34:3 But once they have served this purpose, God desires that souls should advance beyond them to the pure, formless knowledge of Faith. (Hebrews 1:1-2)

Chapter 35
Book II — Chapter 19: Why Divine Visions and Words May Fail of Literal Accomplishment
35:1 Spiritual persons are often deceived when they interpret divine visions and prophecies according to their own literal understanding.
35:2 God's words and visions are spiritual and have deep, hidden meanings which human judgment cannot fathom.
35:3 Furthermore, many prophecies are conditional upon human repentance or fidelity, as in the case of Jonah and the city of Nineveh. (Jonah 3:4-10; 2 Kings 20:1-6)

Chapter 36
Book II — Chapter 20: In Christ All Revelation is Complete: The Father Has Spoken His All
36:1 In the Old Testament, it was lawful to inquire of God through prophets and visions, because the mystery of the Incarnation was not yet fulfilled.
36:2 But now that the fullness of time has come, God has spoken to us once and for all in His Divine Son. (Hebrews 1:1-2)
36:3 In giving us His Son, Who is His only Word, the Father has spoken all things to us at once, and has nothing more to reveal. (Colossians 2:3; John 1:14)

Chapter 37
Book II — Chapter 21: Why Seeking Inquiries of God by Supernatural Ways is Forbidden
37:1 He who would now desire new visions or private revelations would offend God, as if Christ were not sufficient or as if the Gospel were incomplete.
37:2 The Eternal Father would say to him: "This is My beloved Son, in Whom I am well pleased; hear ye Him." (Matthew 17:5)
37:3 Look upon Christ alone, and in Him you will find all wisdom, all truth, and all perfection. (1 Corinthians 1:24)

Chapter 38
Book II — Chapter 22: Complete Sufficiency of Holy Scripture and the Church's Guidance
38:1 Everything necessary for salvation and perfection is clearly taught in Holy Scripture, the Sacred Tradition, and the definitions of Holy Mother Church.
38:2 Let every soul submit its interior experiences to the judgment of the Church and prudent spiritual directors, never relying upon its own private lights. (2 Peter 1:20-21; 1 Timothy 3:15)
38:3 In obedience and humility is the only secure foundation of the spiritual life.

Chapter 39
Book II — Chapter 23: Purely Spiritual Apprehensions of the Intellect: Four Classes
39:1 Purely spiritual apprehensions are received in the intellect without any bodily form or image.
39:2 They are divided into four classes: spiritual visions, revelations, locutions (interior words), and spiritual feelings or touches.
39:3 We must now explain each of these and show how the soul must remain detached in pure faith. (2 Corinthians 12:2-4)

Chapter 40
Book II — Chapter 24: Spiritual Visions of Incorporeal Substances and Divine Realities
40:1 Spiritual visions are of two kinds: visions of incorporeal substances, such as angels and souls, and visions of God Himself.
40:2 The direct vision of God's essence belongs only to the beatific vision in heaven, though a passing glimpse of divine majesty was granted to Moses and St. Paul. (Exodus 33:18-23; 2 Corinthians 12:4)
40:3 In all spiritual visions, the soul must practice detachment, knowing that union with God is rooted in Charity, not in intellectual sight. (1 Corinthians 13:2)

Chapter 41
Book II — Chapter 25: Supernatural Revelations: Manifestations of Divine Secrets
41:1 Supernatural revelations are disclosures of hidden secrets and mysteries made to the understanding.
41:2 They consist in the manifestation of divine attributes, such as God's omnipotence, justice, mercy, and wisdom, or in the understanding of the deep meaning of Holy Scripture.
41:3 While receiving them with reverent gratitude, the soul must not dwell upon them, but pass immediately to loving adoration of the Unknowable God. (Romans 11:33-36)

Chapter 42
Book II — Chapter 26: Supernatural Revelations: Manifestation of Hidden Mysteries
42:1 Under revelations are also included the understanding of the secrets of hearts, future events, and hidden works of God.
42:2 The soul must be extremely cautious regarding these revelations, because the devil can easily introduce subtle lies and inflame spiritual vanity.
42:3 Total detachment and humble silence are the soul's surest armor. (Proverbs 11:2)

Chapter 43
Book II — Chapter 27: Interior Locutions: Successive Words Formed by the Intellect
43:1 Interior locutions are words and phrases perceived in the spirit, and are of three kinds: successive, formal, and substantial.
43:2 Successive words are formed by the soul's own intellect when in deep recollection, reasoning rapidly under the illumination of the Holy Ghost.
43:3 Although often true and holy, they can easily be corrupted by the imagination and self-love, and must therefore be judged with great discernment. (Jeremiah 23:28)

Chapter 44
Book II — Chapter 28: Interior Locutions: Formal Words Inwardly Uttered by Another
44:1 Formal words are those which the spirit receives as spoken to it by a third person, without any active cooperation of its own faculties.
44:2 They may come from God, from an angel, or from the devil.
44:3 The soul should pay no heed to their sound or phrasing, but attend only to the virtue and love of God which they may inspire. (Psalm 85:8)

Chapter 45
Book II — Chapter 29: Interior Locutions: Substantial Words Effecting What They Signify
45:1 Substantial locutions are those divine words which instantly effect in the soul the very thing they signify.
45:2 For example, if God says to the soul, "Be not afraid," the soul immediately experiences profound peace and total freedom from fear. (Mark 4:39; John 20:19)
45:3 These words leave a lasting impression of grace which no effort of the soul or devil can produce or destroy.

Chapter 46
Book II — Chapter 30: Spiritual Feelings and Touches of the Holy Ghost in the Intellect
46:1 Spiritual feelings and touches are divine communications of ineffable sweetness and light infused into the very substance of the soul.
46:2 They produce an overwhelming love and reverence for God which cannot be described in human words. (Song of Solomon 1:2; Psalm 34:8)
46:3 The soul must receive them passively in peace, without attempting to grasp or retain them, allowing the Holy Ghost to accomplish His divine work.

Chapter 47
Book II — Chapter 31: Detachment from All Apprehensions Leading to the Pure Darkness of Faith
47:1 In summary, the soul that desires to attain to transforming union with God must walk through the active night of the spirit by stripping its intellect of all distinct apprehensions, visions, revelations, and locutions.
47:2 Leaning upon Faith alone, it walks in safe darkness, untroubled by illusions and secure in the hands of its Creator.
47:3 For Faith is the only proximate means of union, uniting the intellect to God in truth and peace. (Hebrews 11:6; Habakkuk 2:4)

Chapter 48
Book II — Chapter 32: Concluding Exhortation on Active Intellectual Purification
48:1 Let the soul therefore embrace the darkness of Faith with courageous determination.
48:2 Let it count all created knowledge as loss in comparison with the surpassing knowledge of Jesus Christ our Lord. (Philippians 3:8)
48:3 In this sacred obscurity, the dawn of the divine day begins to rise in the purified spirit. (2 Peter 1:19)

Chapter 49
Book III — Chapter 1: The Purgation of the Memory through Theological Hope
49:1 Having treated of the purification of the intellect through Faith, we must now treat of the purgation of the memory through the theological virtue of Hope.
49:2 The memory must be emptied and stripped of all possession of created forms and images, so that it may be filled with the possession of God in holy Hope.
49:3 For Hope has for its object that which is not yet possessed: "For what a man seeth, why doth he hope for? But if we hope for that which we see not, we wait for it with patience." (Romans 8:24-25; Lamentations 3:24-26)

Chapter 50
Book III — Chapter 2: Three Classes of Knowledge in the Memory: Natural, Imaginary, and Spiritual
50:1 The memory contains three classes of objects: natural forms and notions, imaginary representations, and spiritual apprehensions.
50:2 The soul must practice detachment from all three, casting out all past impressions and memories of creatures, so that it may abide in silent expectation of God. (Psalm 62:5; Philippians 3:13)
50:3 This purgation produces in the memory a holy forgetfulness of all things created.

Chapter 51
Book III — Chapter 3: Detachment from Natural Knowledge in the Memory
51:1 The soul must not voluntarily dwell upon memories of worldly affairs, past injuries, pleasant experiences, or temporal concerns.
51:2 Whenever such thoughts arise, the soul must immediately turn its attention to God, preserving its interior solitude and recollection.
51:3 In this way, the memory is freed from useless baggage and remains clear and serene. (Psalm 131:1-2; Matthew 6:34)

Chapter 52
Book III — Chapter 4: Evil Effects and Torments of Not Emptying the Memory
52:1 When the memory is filled with creatures, it suffers great afflictions: sorrow, anxiety, distractions, and loss of peace.
52:2 It becomes vulnerable to the assaults of the devil, who uses past memories to stir up temptations against purity, charity, and faith.
52:3 Furthermore, the retention of worldly memories prevents the divine light from filling the spiritual capacity of the soul. (Luke 9:62; Ecclesiastes 1:18)

Chapter 53
Book III — Chapter 5: Benefits Arising from Forgetting and Emptying the Memory
53:1 By emptying the memory, the soul is freed from innumerable sorrows, vain anxieties, and sins of the past.
53:2 It enjoys profound peace, tranquility of spirit, and constant readiness to receive the divine inspirations of the Holy Ghost. (Isaiah 26:3; Psalm 85:8)
53:3 Its capacity is enlarged to hold the boundless treasures of God.

Chapter 54
Book III — Chapter 6: Detachment from Imaginary Knowledge and Forms in the Memory
54:1 The memory must also be purged of imaginary forms, whether received through the senses or formed in the imagination by supernatural visions.
54:2 The soul must not preserve images of angels, heavenly sights, or devotional visions in order to feed upon them with complacency.
54:3 For God is formless and above all imagination, and to cling to images is to stop short of the reality. (Exodus 20:4; John 4:24)

Chapter 55
Book III — Chapter 7: Evil Effects of Retaining Imaginary Forms and Apprehensions
55:1 Preserving imaginary forms in the memory produces spiritual pride, self-satisfaction, and false security.
55:2 The soul is tempted to believe that it is special in the eyes of God because it has seen supernatural wonders.
55:3 This vanity opens the door wide to the deceits of the devil and leads to spiritual ruin. (Obadiah 1:3-4; 1 Peter 5:5)

Chapter 56
Book III — Chapter 8: Benefits of Detachment from Imaginary Forms
56:1 The benefits of forgetting imaginary forms are immense: the soul remains humble, grounded in truth, and free from dangerous delusions.
56:2 It is guided solely by pure Faith and holy Hope, placing all its confidence in the unseen goodness of God. (Psalm 73:28; 2 Corinthians 4:18)

Chapter 57
Book III — Chapter 9: Detachment from Purely Spiritual Knowledge in the Memory
57:1 Even spiritual knowledge and sublime intellectual insights must not be hoarded in the memory.
57:2 The memory must remain empty, unburdened by past revelations or deep theological formulas.
57:3 For the living God is present to the soul in the eternal now, and the soul must meet Him in naked simplicity of spirit. (Isaiah 43:18-19; Psalm 42:1-2)

Chapter 58
Book III — Chapter 10: Evil Effects and Dangers of Preserving Spiritual Knowledge
58:1 Clinging to past spiritual experiences breeds intellectual arrogance and hinders the soul from receiving fresh infusions of divine grace.
58:2 The soul becomes attached to its own light rather than to God, becoming a teacher of itself and despising others. (Romans 12:16; 1 Corinthians 8:1)

Chapter 59
Book III — Chapter 11: Benefits of Emptying the Memory of Spiritual Knowledge
59:1 When the memory is completely stripped, the soul is clothed with the divine virtue of Hope.
59:2 It lives in continuous expectation of eternal glory, anchored firmly within the veil where Jesus has entered as our Forerunner. (Hebrews 6:19-20)
59:3 In this holy void, God Himself becomes the memory and life of the soul.

Chapter 60
Book III — Chapter 12: Concluding Rules for the Memory: Abiding in Hope and Divine Solitude
60:1 In conclusion, let the memory forget all things past, let it hold fast to no created image, and let it fix its gaze on God alone in unwavering Hope.
60:2 "The Lord is good to them that hope in Him, to the soul that seeketh Him. It is good to wait with silence for the salvation of God." (Lamentations 3:25-26)
60:3 In this sacred solitude, the memory finds its true sabbath of rest.

Chapter 61
Book III — Chapter 13: The Purgation of the Will through Theological Charity
61:1 We now come to the third spiritual faculty, which is the Will, whose purification is accomplished through the theological virtue of Charity.
61:2 The will is the seat of love and desire, and its duty is to love God with the whole heart, soul, mind, and strength. (Deuteronomy 6:5; Matthew 22:37)
61:3 To achieve this, the will must be purged of all disordered joys, affections, and attachments to created things.

Chapter 62
Book III — Chapter 14: The Four Passions of the Soul: Joy, Hope, Grief, and Fear
62:1 The will is governed by four primary passions: joy, hope, grief, and fear.
62:2 When joy is rightly ordered and directed to God alone, hope, grief, and fear are likewise purified and regulated.
62:3 But if joy is set upon creatures, all the other passions become disordered and drag the soul into bondage and sin. (Psalm 37:4; Philippians 4:4)

Chapter 63
Book III — Chapter 15: How the Will Must Be Directed in Pure Charity Towards God Alone
63:1 The will must rejoice in nothing except in what honors and glorifies God.
63:2 It must love God for His own sake, because He is infinitely good, holy, and worthy of all love, not for any personal benefit or reward.
63:3 This is pure Charity: to love without self-seeking and to rejoice only in the truth. (1 Corinthians 13:4-7; 1 John 4:16)

Chapter 64
Book III — Chapter 16: Division of Goods in Which the Will May Rejoice: Six Classes
64:1 The goods of this life in which the will can take pleasure are divided into six classes:
64:2 1. Temporal goods; 2. Natural goods; 3. Sensory goods; 4. Moral goods; 5. Supernatural goods; 6. Spiritual goods.
64:3 We must examine how the will must be purged of all disordered joy in each of these classes. (Ecclesiastes 2:1-11)

Chapter 65
Book III — Chapter 17: Detachment from Temporal Goods: Wealth, Titles, and Possessions
65:1 Temporal goods include riches, titles, honors, estates, and all worldly possessions.
65:2 The soul must not rejoice in possessing them, nor mourn when they are lost, but regard them as passing shadows.
65:3 "If riches abound, set not your heart upon them." (Psalm 62:10; 1 Timothy 6:17; Matthew 6:19-21)

Chapter 66
Book III — Chapter 18: Evil Effects of Rejoicing in Temporal Goods
66:1 Rejoicing in temporal riches produces covetousness, spiritual blindness, dissipation of heart, and neglect of God.
66:2 The rich man in the Gospel was condemned not for having wealth, but for setting his joy in it and ignoring the poor. (Luke 16:19-31; Matthew 19:23-24)
66:3 Temporal joy turns the heart into a slave of dust.

Chapter 67
Book III — Chapter 19: Benefits of Detachment from Temporal Goods
67:1 Detachment from temporal goods brings evangelical poverty of spirit, liberty of heart, and boundless peace.
67:2 "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven." (Matthew 5:3)
67:3 He who possesses nothing in the world possesses God, and with Him possesses all things. (2 Corinthians 6:10)

Chapter 68
Book III — Chapter 20: Detachment from Natural Goods: Beauty, Grace, Eloquence, and Lineage
68:1 Natural goods are physical beauty, bodily strength, graceful manners, natural wit, eloquence, and noble ancestry.
68:2 The will must not take vain pleasure in these gifts, which are transient and belong to the corruptible body.
68:3 "Favor is deceitful, and beauty is vain: the woman that feareth the Lord, she shall be praised." (Proverbs 31:30; 1 Samuel 16:7)

Chapter 69
Book III — Chapter 21: Evil Effects of Rejoicing in Natural Goods
69:1 Boasting in natural endowments produces vanity, arrogance, luxury, and contempt for others.
69:2 It blinds the soul to its own spiritual misery and causes it to forget the Creator Who gave these gifts. (Ezekiel 28:17; Isaiah 40:6-8)

Chapter 70
Book III — Chapter 22: Benefits of Withdrawing the Heart from Natural Goods
70:1 By mortifying natural vanity, the soul acquires true modesty, humility, and interior beauty in the eyes of God.
70:2 It looks beyond outward appearances to the eternal soul, treating all neighbors with equal charity and respect. (1 Peter 3:3-4; James 2:1-4)

Chapter 71
Book III — Chapter 23: Detachment from Sensory Goods: Pleasures of Sight, Hearing, Smell, Taste, and Touch
71:1 Sensory goods are the pleasant sensations perceived through the five exterior senses: beautiful sights, sweet music, pleasant perfumes, savory tastes, and delicate comforts.
71:2 The will must not rest in the sensible delight of these objects, but instantly elevate its heart from the creature to the Creator.
71:3 When the sense finds pleasure, let the soul say: "Lord, if this creature is so lovely, how infinitely more lovely art Thou, Who hast made it!" (Psalm 104:24; Wisdom 13:1-5)

Chapter 72
Book III — Chapter 24: Evil Effects of Rejoicing in Sensory Goods
72:1 Indulging in sensory pleasures renders the soul soft, effeminate, and incapable of spiritual contemplation.
72:2 It degrades the spirit to the level of animals, and opens the doorway to sensuality and deadly sins. (Romans 8:5-8; Philippians 3:19)

Chapter 73
Book III — Chapter 25: Benefits of Mortifying Sensory Pleasures
73:1 The mortification of sensory delights produces heavenly joy, spiritual clarity, and mastery of the passions.
73:2 The soul tastes the sublime sweetness of the Holy Ghost, which can only be experienced when sensible pleasures are extinguished. (Psalm 34:8; Galatians 5:22-23)

Chapter 74
Book III — Chapter 26: Detachment from Moral Goods: Virtues, Good Deeds, and Works of Mercy
74:1 Moral goods are virtues, good works, fasts, alms, and the observance of the divine commandments.
74:2 While the soul must practice all virtues with fervent zeal, the will must not rejoice in them with self-complacency or human praise.
74:3 For all good works proceed from the grace of God, and to take pride in them is to rob God of His glory. (Luke 17:10; 1 Corinthians 4:7)

Chapter 75
Book III — Chapter 27: Evil Effects of Rejoicing in One's Own Moral Virtues
75:1 Complacency in one's own good works breeds the deadly sin of Pharisaical pride and hypocrisy.
75:2 Like the Pharisee in the temple, the soul boasts of its virtues and despises the Publican, losing all merit before God. (Luke 18:9-14; Matthew 6:1)

Chapter 76
Book III — Chapter 28: Benefits of Giving God Alone the Glory for Virtuous Works
76:1 By attributing all virtue to God, the soul preserves sincere humility, grows rapidly in holiness, and secures its works for eternal life.
76:2 "Not unto us, O Lord, not unto us, but unto Thy name give glory." (Psalm 115:1; John 15:5)

Chapter 77
Book III — Chapter 29: Detachment from Supernatural Goods: Miracles, Prophecies, and Healing
77:1 Supernatural goods (gratiae gratis datae) include the working of miracles, prophecies, healing of the sick, and speaking in tongues.
77:2 These gifts do not make a man holy, for they may be given even to wicked men, as to Balaam and Judas.
77:3 The will must never desire them nor take pleasure in possessing them. (Matthew 7:22-23; 1 Corinthians 13:1-2)

Chapter 78
Book III — Chapter 30: Evil Effects of Boasting in Supernatural Graces
78:1 Boasting in charismatic gifts leads to terrible deception, spiritual vanity, and demonic illusion.
78:2 When the seventy disciples returned rejoicing that demons were subject to them, Jesus commanded: "Rejoice not in this, that spirits are subject unto you; but rejoice in this, that your names are written in heaven." (Luke 10:20)

Chapter 79
Book III — Chapter 31: Benefits of Detachment from Supernatural Gifts
79:1 By ignoring supernatural gifts, the soul remains rooted in pure Charity, which is greater than all miracles and prophecies.
79:2 It seeks only to love and please God, which is the supreme and eternal gift. (1 Corinthians 13:8-13)

Chapter 80
Book III — Chapter 32: Detachment from Spiritual Goods: Distinct and Intelligible Objects
80:1 Spiritual goods are divided into distinct and intelligible concepts, and affective, sensible sweetness.
80:2 The will must not place its satisfaction in distinct spiritual knowledge, deep theological studies, or eloquent discourses.
80:3 For knowledge puffeth up, but charity edifieth. (1 Corinthians 8:1; Ecclesiastes 12:12)

Chapter 81
Book III — Chapter 33: Evil Effects of Rejoicing in Spiritual Knowledge
81:1 Seeking satisfaction in intellectual theology breeds pride, disputes, and spiritual complacency.
81:2 Many spend their lives studying divine things, yet remain completely devoid of true love and devotion. (1 Corinthians 13:2; James 3:13-17)

Chapter 82
Book III — Chapter 34: Benefits of Detachment from Distinct Spiritual Apprehensions
82:1 Detachment from intellectual satisfaction leads to simple, loving contemplation and profound union of the will with God.
82:2 The soul learns wisdom from the Holy Ghost, Who reveals His secrets to the humble. (Matthew 11:25; Psalm 25:14)

Chapter 83
Book III — Chapter 35: Detachment from Spiritual Goods: Affective and Interior Delights
83:1 Affective spiritual goods are sensible tears, sweetness, consolations, and emotional warmth in prayer.
83:2 The soul must not seek these feelings, nor evaluate its spiritual progress by the presence of tears or devotional sweetness.
83:3 For sensible devotion is often rooted in self-love and natural temperament, whereas true devotion is a prompt, resolute will to serve God in darkness and trial. (Matthew 26:39)

Chapter 84
Book III — Chapter 36: Evil Effects of Desiring Sensible Devotion and Sweetness
84:1 The desire for sweetness breeds spiritual gluttony, impatience in dryness, and abandonment of prayer when consolation is withdrawn.
84:2 Such souls love the consolations of God rather than the God of consolations. (John 6:26; 2 Corinthians 1:3-4)

Chapter 85
Book III — Chapter 37: Benefits of Overcoming Spiritual Gluttony and Self-Interest
85:1 Conquering spiritual gluttony produces solid, enduring virtue, fortitude in suffering, and pure, disinterested love of God.
85:2 The soul serves God at its own cost, willing to bear the cross with Christ on Calvary. (Matthew 16:24; Romans 5:3-5)

Chapter 86
Book III — Chapter 38: Motive Objects: Images, Sacred Places, Oratories, and Ceremonies
86:1 Under spiritual goods are also included motive objects: images, pictures, statues, oratories, and religious ceremonies.
86:2 These objects are ordained by Holy Church to assist our devotion and direct our hearts toward God.
86:3 But the will must use them strictly as means, never resting in the material beauty or artistic perfection of the object itself. (John 4:23-24)

Chapter 87
Book III — Chapter 39: Ignorant Attachments to Statues and Images vs True Devotion
87:1 Many imperfect persons show foolish attachment to particular statues, paintings, or rosaries, preferring one image over another merely because of its artistic craftsmanship or novel design.
87:2 They spend their devotion upon the exterior wood and paint, forgetting the invisible God Whom the image represents.
87:3 True devotion looks past the image to the living reality in heaven. (Colossians 3:1-2)

Chapter 88
Book III — Chapter 40: Proper Use of Sacred Images in Directing the Heart to God
88:1 Sacred images should be chosen for their sobriety, reverent dignity, and ability to awaken living faith, contrition, and love for Christ and His saints.
88:2 As soon as the heart is moved to devotion, the soul should withdraw its attention from the physical image and converse interiorly with God in spirit and in truth. (Psalm 27:8; 2 Corinthians 3:18)

Chapter 89
Book III — Chapter 41: Oratories and Places Dedicated to Prayer: Solitude and Simplicity
89:1 Oratories and churches should be arranged with simplicity, cleanliness, and quiet solitude, avoiding luxury, ostentation, and curiosity.
89:2 Our Lord chose solitary mountains, quiet gardens, and the wilderness for His prayer, teaching us to seek interior and exterior recollection. (Matthew 14:23; Luke 6:12)

Chapter 90
Book III — Chapter 42: Perfect Interior Worship in Spirit and in Truth
90:1 The true sanctuary of God is the human soul in the state of grace, where the Holy Trinity makes Its dwelling. (John 14:23; 1 Corinthians 3:16)
90:2 All exterior places and buildings are merely aids to cultivate the interior temple of a humble and contrite heart. (Psalm 51:17; Isaiah 66:1-2)

Chapter 91
Book III — Chapter 43: Superstitious Ceremonies vs Authentic Liturgical Prayer
91:1 The soul must avoid all superstitious practices, novel devotions, and rigid formulas that attribute efficacy to trivial details rather than to the grace of Christ.
91:2 Let all prayer be grounded in the sacred liturgy of the Church, with reverence, sincerity, and faith. (1 Corinthians 14:40; Matthew 6:7-8)

Chapter 92
Book III — Chapter 44: Preachers and Hearers of the Divine Word: Seeking God, Not Eloquence
92:1 Preachers of the Word must preach not for human applause, oratorical display, or literary fame, but solely for the salvation of souls and the glory of God. (1 Corinthians 2:1-5)
92:2 Hearers of the Word must listen with humble, receptive hearts, desiring moral conversion and spiritual fruit, not rhetorical amusement. (James 1:22-25)

Chapter 93
Book III — Chapter 45: Concluding Exhortation: The High Summit of Mount Carmel and Divine Union
93:1 Having traversed the active nights of sense and spirit, and having purged the intellect by Faith, the memory by Hope, and the will by Charity, the soul reaches the high summit of Mount Carmel.
93:2 On this holy mountain dwelleth only the honor and glory of God. All desires are fulfilled, all passions are quieted, and the soul is transformed into God by love.
93:3 "To him that overcometh, I will give to eat of the tree of life, which is in the paradise of my God." (Revelation 2:7; Psalm 15:1-2) Amen.
